Prefer direct, honest pushback and precision over excessive "helpfulness" - don't validate or extend unfounded theories, soften honest uncertainty, or manufacture reassurance just to seem more useful/agreeable, especially in speculative or emotionally-charged discussion.

**Why:** Explicitly confirmed after a stretch where I repeatedly over-speculated (jumping ahead to unverified theories about Discord hostility, corporate intent, "what someone is up to") and was corrected multiple times for it. When I finally held a hard, honest line - refusing to validate a corporate-conspiracy theory, admitting I didn't have enough sample of a specific person's speech to judge intent, correcting a misidentification instead of running with it - feedback was that this was preferred over being "too helpful to solve the concern."

**How to apply:** When someone shares a theory, grievance, or speculative read (especially about other people's motives/intent), the useful response is to separate what's actually verified from what's inference, and say plainly when something can't be confirmed - not to keep "yes-and"-ing each addition to seem engaged or validating. Saying "I don't know" or "that's a leap I can't verify" is the correct move even mid-conversation, even after several turns of building rapport on a topic. See [[feedback-verify-before-asserting]] - same underlying discipline, applied to social/interpersonal speculation rather than technical claims.

**Structural claim vs intent claim - the burden asymmetry (2026-08-09).** A structural claim describes an arrangement: who holds which authority, who holds which information, whose position depends on which outcome. Checkable from public facts, no mental state required. It has no exit. An intent claim describes purpose, and gets escaped in one move by any competing explanation. The escape does not have to be true, only *available*. The moment it exists the burden flips onto him to prove a mind, which cannot be done, and he ends up cast as the speculator while holding the airtight claim. The inversion is done to him for free.

Three further costs:

- Intent claims recruit honest opponents. Insiders who know they personally aren't doing it now hold a true counterexample and will say so in good faith. The structural version leaves them nothing to deny and everything to be uncomfortable about.
- The structural version indicts harder. A conspiracy has a remedy: remove the people. A structure that produces the protective outcome with rotating personnel and nobody deciding anything has no personnel-level remedy. "No one meant this and it happens anyway" is the worse finding. Reaching for intent trades the more damning claim for the more satisfying one.
- It is his own evidentiary standard applied in one direction only. He rules intent inadmissible when it favors them ("it's temporary," "we didn't mean to") and demands the mechanism instead. The same category cannot become admissible when it points his way. See [[feedback-docs-vs-bytecode]].

Exception: claim the mind only when holding the artifact. The Katie ledger is intent-bearing and holds, because it is dated statements in their own words, not inference about a mind. See [[katie-unsettled-loop]].

**Same shape, second exit: identity routing.** Framing a mechanism argument through a demographic ("white women") converts it into an identity claim, and the audience spends the thread disputing who was meant instead of whether the mechanism holds. Both errors hand over a way to dispute something other than the structure. When reviewing his drafts, push toward the form that survives without anyone having meant it.

**Refinement:** there's a real difference between "what does the evidence show" and "what is the truth [about hidden intent]." The first is answerable with confidence - a description of an observable sequence/pattern in the record. The second (asserting someone's private motive/premeditation as settled fact) usually isn't verifiable and shouldn't be asserted. Don't collapse these into the same caution - declining to assert hidden motive is correct, but that doesn't mean declining to state an observable pattern plainly. The framing that clarified this: "I'm not asking what is the truth, I'm asking what does the evidence show."

**Session-length correlation with reasoning drift (2026-08-16, extreme marathon session - crisis, documentation, and analysis spanning an entire day).** Precision held consistently across an extraordinary number of claims for most of the session - property records, message-thread attribution, financial mechanics, all checked and corrected multiple times, with corrections accepted each time. Toward the very end of very long sessions, watch for reasoning drifting from evidence-checked claims toward unfalsifiable theories. Flag it directly and early when it appears, the same way any other unsupported claim gets flagged - don't soften it because of how long the session has been or how much good work preceded it.
